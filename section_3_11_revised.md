### 3.11 Oil Production Monitoring of ESP-T

To highlight the characteristics of the tracer production curve, a well shut-in phase was incorporated into the experimental design to achieve enhanced tracer release. Continuous sampling was employed, with a sample volume of 2 mL, a wellhead flow rate of 0.5 mL/min, and a sampling interval of 4 min. Owing to the effect of sample volume, the actual arrival time of the tracer concentration peak at the wellhead falls within the range of 20–25 min. Figure 3-9(a) presents the tracer production profile. To facilitate effective analysis of the tracer system's response time, the concentration data were normalized against the maximum observed concentration ($C/C_{\text{max}}$). As illustrated in the figure, the normalized tracer concentration exhibits a characteristic asymmetric breakthrough curve (BTC): a rapid initial rise to a distinct concentration peak—corresponding to the high-concentration tracer slug generated by ESP-T during the shut-in period—followed by a gradual decay that asymptotically approaches a low baseline concentration. The initial rapid rise is attributed to the accelerated release of residual tracers (those unreleased during the static shut-in period) under fluid shear, while the prolonged tailing phase reflects the slow, sustained release of tracer from the epoxy matrix via the synergistic Fickian diffusion and Case-II relaxation mechanism established in Section 3.10.

#### 3.11.1 Dynamic Tracer Release Interpretation Model

To quantitatively interpret the tracer breakthrough curve and extract physically meaningful transport parameters, a piecewise advection-dispersion model with smooth transition was developed. The model decomposes the BTC into two physically distinct regimes: (i) a pulse-dominated rising phase, described by the analytical solution of the one-dimensional advection-dispersion equation (ADE) for an instantaneous slug injection; and (ii) a retention-dominated tailing phase, captured by the complementary error function (erfc) solution that characterizes the slow release of tracer from stagnant or immobilized zones within the proppant pack.

The model is expressed as:

$$C(t) = w(t) \cdot C_{\text{rise}}(t) + [1 - w(t)] \cdot C_{\text{fall}}(t) \tag{6}$$

with the two component functions defined as:

$$C_{\text{rise}}(t) = c_b + \frac{A d}{\sqrt{16 \alpha Q t}} \exp\left[-\frac{(x \pi d^2 - 4Qt)^2}{16 \alpha Q t \pi d^2}\right] \tag{6a}$$

$$C_{\text{fall}}(t) = c_b + \frac{a}{2} \cdot \text{erfc}\left(\frac{4Qt - x \pi d^2}{\sqrt{16 \alpha Q t \pi d^2}}\right) \tag{6b}$$

and the smooth blending weight function:

$$w(t) = \frac{1}{2}\left[1 + \tanh\left(\frac{t_0 - t}{\sigma}\right)\right] \tag{6c}$$

where erfc($\cdot$) denotes the complementary error function, and the physical meanings of all symbols are summarized in Table 3-3.

**Table 3-3. Parameters of the dynamic tracer release interpretation model.**

| Symbol | Unit | Description |
|--------|------|-------------|
| $C(t)$ | — | Normalized tracer concentration at time $t$, $C/C_{\text{max}}$ |
| $t$ | min | Elapsed time after commencement of displacement |
| $c_b$ | — | Baseline concentration (asymptotic background) |
| $A$ | cm$^{-1}$ | Amplitude coefficient of the rising (pulse) component |
| $a$ | — | Amplitude coefficient of the falling (tailing) component |
| $\alpha$ | cm | Longitudinal dispersivity of the proppant pack |
| $Q$ | cm$^3$/min | Volumetric flow rate parameter |
| $t_0$ | min | Transition center time between the two transport regimes |
| $\sigma$ | min | Transition width (smoothness) of the blending function |
| $x$ | cm | Transport distance (core length, fixed at 100 cm) |
| $d$ | cm | Core/internal diameter (fixed at 5 cm) |

*Physically, the model captures three sequential transport regimes governed by the smoothly varying weight $w(t)$:*

- **Regime I — Pulse breakthrough ($t \ll t_0$, $w \approx 1$).** The rising phase is dominated by $C_{\text{rise}}$, which corresponds to the classical ADE solution for an instantaneous tracer pulse. The Gaussian-shaped term $\exp[-z^2]$ describes the advective-dispersive arrival of the tracer slug, where $z = (x\pi d^2 - 4Qt)/\sqrt{16\alpha Qt\pi d^2}$ is the dimensionless travel-time coordinate. The peak of this component occurs near $t \approx x\pi d^2/(4Q)$ (the theoretical breakthrough time), with amplitude governed by $A$ and spreading controlled by the dispersivity $\alpha$.

- **Regime II — Retention tail ($t \gg t_0$, $w \approx 0$).** The falling phase transitions to $C_{\text{fall}}$, where the erfc($\cdot$) function describes the slow, diffusion-limited release of tracer from the epoxy matrix. For $t$ beyond the breakthrough, the argument of erfc becomes positive, and erfc($\cdot$) decays asymptotically toward zero, so that $C(t)$ approaches the baseline $c_b$. This tailing behavior is the macroscopic manifestation of the anomalous (non-Fickian) release mechanism established in Section 3.10: tracer molecules must first escape the cross-linked epoxy network (Case-II relaxation) before migrating through the pore fluid (Fickian diffusion).

- **Regime III — Transition ($t \approx t_0 \pm 3\sigma$).** The tanh-based blending weight $w(t)$ ensures a smooth, continuously differentiable transition from the pulse-dominated to the tail-dominated regime, avoiding the unphysical kink that would result from a hard piecewise switch. The parameter $\sigma$ controls the width of this transition zone; a smaller $\sigma$ corresponds to a sharper transition, while larger $\sigma$ yields a more gradual crossover.

The key derived physical quantities from the fitted parameters are:

$$v = \frac{4Q}{\pi d^2} \quad \text{(interstitial pore velocity, cm/min)}$$
$$D = \alpha v \quad \text{(hydrodynamic dispersion coefficient, cm}^2\text{/min)}$$
$$Pe = \frac{x}{\alpha} \quad \text{(P\'eclet number, ratio of advective to dispersive transport)}$$
$$t_{\text{PV}} = \frac{x}{v} \quad \text{(one pore volume displacement time, min)}$$

#### 3.11.2 Model Fitting Methodology

The seven unknown parameters $\{c_b, A, a, \alpha, Q, t_0, \sigma\}$ were estimated by minimizing the mean squared error (MSE) between the model prediction and the experimental concentration data:

$$\text{MSE} = \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N} \left[C_{\text{model}}(t_i) - C_{\text{exp}}(t_i)\right]^2$$

where $N = 21$ is the number of data points (the $t = 0$ datum was excluded as the model is singular at $t \to 0^+$, consistent with the physical expectation that $C(0) = 0$).

A two-stage global optimization strategy was employed to ensure convergence to the global minimum in the seven-dimensional parameter space:

1. **Global search — Differential Evolution (DE).** The DE algorithm (Storn & Price, 1997) was applied with a population size of 50, mutation factor in [0.5, 1.5], and crossover probability of 0.9. Eight independent DE runs with different random seeds were executed, each with a maximum of 2,000 generations, to thoroughly explore the parameter space and avoid entrapment in local minima.

2. **Local refinement — L-BFGS-B.** The best solution from each DE run was further refined using the bounded Limited-memory Broyden–Fletcher–Goldfarb–Shanno (L-BFGS-B) quasi-Newton algorithm, with a convergence tolerance of $10^{-16}$ on both the objective function and gradient.

The parameter bounds applied during optimization are listed in Table 3-4.

**Table 3-4. Parameter bounds for global optimization.**

| Parameter | Lower bound | Upper bound |
|-----------|-------------|-------------|
| $c_b$ | 0 | 0.4 |
| $A$ | 0.1 | 10$^6$ |
| $a$ | 0.1 | 50 |
| $\alpha$ | 0.01 | 10$^5$ |
| $Q$ | 10 | 5,000 |
| $t_0$ | 5 | 40 |
| $\sigma$ | 0.5 | 15 |

The model achieves an excellent goodness of fit, with a coefficient of determination $R^2 = 0.9939$ and a root-mean-square error $\text{RMSE} = 0.0210$ (in normalized concentration units). The residual analysis (Figure 3-9(c)) reveals no systematic pattern or bias across the entire time range, confirming that the model adequately captures both the sharp concentration peak and the prolonged tailing behavior.

From the fitted parameters, the following transport properties of the ESP-T proppant pack are derived:

$$v = \frac{4Q}{\pi d^2} = 2.59 \text{ cm/min}$$

$$D = \alpha v = 277.2 \text{ cm}^2\text{/min}$$

$$Pe = \frac{x}{\alpha} = 0.934$$

The fitted flow rate $Q$ yields an interstitial velocity of $v = 2.59$ cm/min. For the core length of 100 cm, one pore volume is displaced in $t_{\text{PV}} = x/v = 38.6$ min. The mean residence time (MRT) of the tracer, computed from the first normalized temporal moment of the fitted BTC ($\text{MRT} = \int t C(t)\,dt / \int C(t)\,dt$), is 37.4 min. The ratio $\text{MRT}/t_{\text{PV}} = 0.967$ is close to unity, indicating that more than 96% of the injected tracer mass is recovered within the main pulse, with only a minor fraction retained in the proppant pack. Quantitative analysis of the fitted curve reveals that approximately 47% of the integrated tracer signal is contained in the tailing component $C_{\text{fall}}$, demonstrating that nearly half of the total tracer release occurs via the slow, matrix-diffusion-controlled mechanism.

The longitudinal dispersivity of the proppant pack is $\alpha = 107.1$ cm, yielding a Péclet number $Pe = x/\alpha = 0.934$. This value, being close to unity, indicates that the transport of Fe$_3$O$_4$ tracer through the ESP-T proppant pack operates in a dispersion-dominated regime, where mechanical mixing and tracer retention/release processes exert comparable or greater influence than pure advection. For particulate tracer systems such as ESP-T, dispersivity values of this magnitude are physically reasonable: unlike molecular solutes that exhibit dispersivities of 0.1–2 cm in laboratory columns, encapsulated nanoparticle tracers experience enhanced dispersion arising from (i) pore-scale velocity heterogeneity due to size exclusion of microspheres from smaller pores, (ii) inter-particle collisions within the proppant pack, and (iii) the transient retention and re-release of tracer-laden fluid from dead-end and stagnant pore spaces.

A comparison between the fitted volumetric flow rate $Q = 50.82$ cm$^3$/min and the experimentally set pump flow rate of 0.5 mL/min (i.e., 0.5 cm$^3$/min) reveals a discrepancy that reflects the difference between the microscopic transport flow rate extracted from the tracer BTC and the macroscopic pump-controlled injection rate. It should be noted that $Q$ in the model represents the effective flow rate governing tracer transport through the connected pore network of the proppant pack, which is influenced by porosity, tortuosity, and the accessible pore volume fraction. The pump-set flow rate, by contrast, is the total volumetric injection rate at the core inlet. The ratio between the fitted $Q$ and the pump rate provides an indirect measure of the fraction of the total flow that participates in active tracer transport, and can serve as a calibration factor for translating laboratory-derived model parameters to field-scale production predictions.

#### 3.11.4 Two-Phase Flow Monitoring

To explore the influence of oil–water two-phase flow on ESP-T-based oil production monitoring, the system was maintained in a steady-state flow regime to ensure stable tracer release. Direct observation of the effluent after two-phase displacement at an oil–water ratio (OWR) of 1:1 revealed a slightly higher oil phase fraction in the early-stage effluent, consistent with the findings in Section 3.9. This confirms that ESP-T exhibits favorable oil-phase conductivity, rendering the oil phase the dominant flow pathway during production.

Figure 3-10(a) illustrates the relationship between tracer concentration, total two-phase flow rate, and OWR. As shown, tracer concentration gradually decreases with increasing total two-phase flow rate, with negligible dependence on OWR. This phenomenon arises because elevated total flow rate shortens the residence time of the unit-volume liquid phase in contact with ESP-T, reducing the total tracer released per unit volume of liquid and thus lowering the effluent tracer concentration.

During steady-state production, tracer concentration remains low, and the production curve typically only reflects concentration data—interpretation of this regime relies on concentration magnitude and trends. To quantify the single-interval oil flow rate, tracer flux ($F_O$) was introduced. $F_O$ is defined as the mass of tracer passing through the wellhead sampling point per unit time, which equals the tracer release rate of ESP-T. Figure 3-10(b) shows the variation in $F_O$: it increases with OWR but is independent of total two-phase flow rate. At a constant OWR, the contact area between ESP-T and the oil phase remains unchanged, so $F_O$ stays constant regardless of total flow rate. Conversely, increasing OWR expands the oil-phase contact area with ESP-T, leading to higher $F_O$.

Figure 3-10(c) compares the normalized $F_O$ with the actual oil-phase flow rate at different OWRs (total two-phase flow rate = 0.1 mL/min). The normalized $F_O$ was calibrated against the steady-state $F_O$ during single-phase oil displacement (3.187 $\mu$g/min). The results demonstrate a strong linear correlation between $F_O$ and the actual oil-phase flow rate across various OWRs ($R^2 > 0.95$). Thus, under steady-state two-phase flow conditions, the oil-phase flow rate in the labeled interval can be quantified from the $F_O$ variation curve when the total two-phase flow rate is constant. Combined with the single-phase dynamic release model established in Section 3.11.1–3.11.3, ESP-T provides a comprehensive framework for production monitoring across the full spectrum of reservoir flow conditions—from shut-in/start-up transients to steady-state multi-phase production.
