[114] The K-P model is a power-law formulation developed by Korsmeyer and Peppas [27], widely employed to characterize release kinetics from controlled-release systems. It captures two key mechanisms: Fickian diffusion, in which substances migrate from high- to low-concentration regions in accordance with Fick's laws, and Case-II relaxation, in which polymer swelling upon contact with the release medium increases internal porosity and facilitates the escape of entrapped substances. For spherical carriers, the diffusion exponent n determines the dominant mechanism: n ≤ 0.43 corresponds to Fickian-diffusion-controlled release; 0.43 < n < 0.85 indicates anomalous transport co-governed by both mechanisms; and n ≥ 0.85 corresponds to Case-II-relaxation-controlled release [28].

All n values fall within 0.45–0.85, confirming that ESP-T release follows an anomalous transport mechanism governed by the synergistic combination of Fickian diffusion and Case-II relaxation. The coefficient of determination (R²) exceeds 0.94 for all test temperatures (Table 3-2), confirming that the K-P model adequately describes ESP-T release kinetics within the experimental duration. We note that the K-P model, as a power-law formulation, lacks an upper asymptote and is strictly valid only for the early-to-intermediate release regime (M_t/M_inf < 0.6); extrapolated C/C0 values at 14 days exceed unity, reflecting this inherent limitation. Accordingly, the model should not be extrapolated beyond the experimental timeframe, and the reported R-squared values pertain to the 0-14 day fitting range.

[118] Based on these results, the release mechanism of ESP-T is inferred as follows: nonpolar solvent molecules (e.g., dodecane) permeate into the cross-linked epoxy network, inducing swelling that generates a dual-state structure consisting of an inner glassy core and an outer gel layer. This swelling reduces polymer chain entanglement, creating expanded transport channels through which tracers diffuse into the external medium. Elevated temperature enhances solvent permeability into the polymer matrix, accelerating the swelling rate and weakening intermolecular forces within the network, thereby enlarging pore dimensions and further promoting tracer diffusion through the matrix.

[126] The interpretation model is derived from the one-dimensional advection-dispersion equation (ADE): ∂C/∂t + v·∂C/∂x = D·∂²C/∂x², where v = 4Q/(πd²) is the mean flow velocity (cm/min) and D = α·v is the longitudinal dispersion coefficient (cm²/min), with Q the effective oil-phase volumetric flow rate (cm³/min), d the tubing inner diameter (cm), and α the longitudinal dispersivity (cm). For a semi-infinite domain with an instantaneous slug of tracer mass M injected at x = 0, t = 0, the classical ADE analytical solution [29] yields a Gaussian pulse: Crise(x,t) = (M/A)/sqrt(4π D t) exp[-(x - v t)2 / (4 D t)], which describes the advective-dispersive transport of the tracer accumulated in the proppant pack during the well shut-in period. For a continuous source maintaining a constant concentration C0 at the inlet, the ADE solution takes the complementary-error-function form: Cfall(x,t) = (C0/2) erfc[(x - v t) / sqrt(4 D t)], which captures the sustained release of residual nano-Fe₃O₄@SA from the epoxy matrix after the main concentration pulse has passed. These two transport regimes—the shut-in slug and the matrix-diffusion-controlled tail—operate simultaneously during production. To construct a physically continuous composite model that avoids the non-differentiable discontinuity of a piecewise step transition, we blend the two analytical solutions via a hyperbolic-tangent weighting function w(t) = ½[1 + tanh((t - t₀)/σ)], where t0 is the crossover center and sigma controls the transition width. The resulting piecewise advection-dispersion model—incorporating both the Gaussian pulse and erfc tail components with smooth tanh transition—The full composite model is then C(t) = cb + w(t)·Crise + [1-w(t)]·Cfall, with the tanh weight smoothly shifting dominance from the Gaussian pulse at early times to the erfc tail at late times, avoiding any artificial discontinuity in the breakthrough curve. [146] The fitted parameters carry several physically significant implications that extend beyond empirical curve-fitting. First, the Peclet number Pe = x/alpha = 0.934 places the transport regime precisely at the transition between advection-dominated and dispersion-dominated behavior. This regime is characteristic of a gradually releasing source: pure advection would produce a sharp, temporally compressed Gaussian pulse, while the comparable dispersion broadens the peak and generates the extended tail that carries 47% of the integrated signal. The near-unity Pe thus constitutes independent physical corroboration of the matrix-diffusion-controlled release mechanism identified in Section 3.6. Second, the tanh blending function provides a physically motivated, continuously differentiable transition between the Gaussian rise and erfc tail regimes. A piecewise step-function transition would introduce a non-physical discontinuity in the first derivative of the concentration profile at the crossover. Third, the close agreement between the pump-set flow rate (0.50 mL/min) and the independently fitted flow rate (0.46 mL/min, 8% relative error) demonstrates that the model parameters carry well-defined physical meaning and can be recovered from the tracer signal alone. Fourth, the 47% erfc tail contribution quantitatively confirms that nearly half of the total detectable tracer signal originates from sustained matrix-diffusion-controlled release rather than from the initial concentration pulse. This establishes that ESP-T-based production monitoring is fundamentally a diffusion-governed, extended-duration process—a conclusion inaccessible from empirical release fitting alone, and one that directly informs the design of sampling protocols and shut-in durations in field deployments. Fifth, the fitted tail-plateau concentration cb, though treated as a constant in the present single-temperature experiment, reflects the steady-state diffusion flux sustained by matrix-diffusion-controlled release. Since Section 3.6 demonstrates that this release process is thermally activated—the K-P rate constant K increases systematically from 0.055 at 30 degC to 0.196 at 120 degC—cb is expected to scale with reservoir temperature. This connection between the transport-model parameter cb and the independently measured release kinetics provides a pathway for temperature-dependent model parameterization in multi-temperature field applications.

[151] During steady-state production, tracer concentrations are inherently low, and the production curve conveys information primarily through the magnitude and temporal trend of the concentration signal. To quantify the single-interval oil flow rate, tracer flux (FO) was introduced. FO is defined as the mass of tracer passing through the wellhead sampling point per unit time, which equals the tracer release rate of ESP-T [8]. Figure 3-9(b) shows the variation in FO: it increases with OWR but is independent of total two-phase flow rate. At a constant OWR, the contact area between ESP-T and the oil phase remains unchanged, so FO stays constant regardless of total flow rate. Conversely, increasing OWR expands the oil-phase contact area with ESP-T, leading to higher FO.

The normalized FO was calibrated against the steady-state FO during single-phase oil displacement (3.187 μg/min). The results demonstrate close agreement between FO and the actual oil-phase flow rate across all OWRs tested. Thus, under steady-state two-phase flow conditions, the oil-phase flow rate in the labeled interval can be quantified from the FO variation curve when the total two-phase flow rate is constant. Several limitations should be noted. First, the piecewise ADE model assumes a single fractured interval and uniform proppant-pack properties; multi-interval interactions and pack heterogeneity may introduce deviations in field applications. Second, the tracer flux calibration relies on steady-state flow; during transient flow regimes (e.g., well start-up, shut-in, or rapid drawdown), the relationship between FO and oil-phase flow rate may not hold. Third, the laboratory-scale validation used dodecane as a model oil; crude oil compositional effects (asphaltene adsorption, viscosity variation) on tracer release kinetics require further investigation. Fourth, at temperatures exceeding 120 °C or in the presence of aggressive formation fluids (high salinity, CO₂, H₂S), the long-term chemical stability of the epoxy matrix and the integrity of stearic acid surface modification warrant additional evaluation. These limitations delineate the current applicability envelope.

[157] 4. Conclusions

[158] The central challenge addressed in this work is the disconnect between tracer release kinetics—measured in batch experiments and fitted with empirical models—and the quantitative interpretation of tracer breakthrough signals at the wellhead. To bridge this gap, we (i) developed a two-component transport model that decomposes tracer breakthrough curves into a Gaussian pulse and an erfc tail, linked by a smooth tanh transition, and (ii) designed an oleophilic epoxy/Fe3O4 tracer proppant (ESP-T) as the enabling material platform for model validation.

[160] On the modeling side, the two-component transport analysis achieves R2 = 0.9939 for single-phase tracer breakthrough curves, with the erfc tail component contributing 47% of the integrated signal. This quantitatively establishes that sustained, matrix-diffusion-controlled release—rather than the initial shut-in concentration pulse—dominates the long-term monitoring signal. The independently fitted flow rate (0.46 mL/min) agrees within 8% of the pump-set value (0.50 mL/min), confirming that the model parameters are physically meaningful rather than purely empirical. The Peclet number (Pe = 0.934) independently corroborates the non-Fickian release mechanism inferred from Korsmeyer-Peppas kinetics (n = 0.45-0.85, R2 > 0.90), demonstrating that two independent analytical approaches converge on the same physical picture. Under steady-state two-phase flow, tracer flux scales predictably with the oil-water ratio and is independent of total flow rate, providing a pathway for translating tracer concentration data into per-interval oil production rates.

[162] The ESP-T proppant designed as the validation platform for this model exhibits a performance profile consistent with downhole deployment requirements. The stearic acid modification of nano-Fe3O4@SA imparts a pronounced oleophilic character: the water contact angle increases from 72.3 deg to 104.6 deg relative to neat epoxy, and oil filtration time is reduced by 66.1% (from 15 min 11 s to 5 min 11 s), while water filtration time increases nearly tenfold. Thermal analysis confirms structural integrity up to 357.27 degC—far above typical downhole temperatures of 80-200 degC—with only trace moisture loss below 200 degC. The bulk density (0.646 g/cm3) remains below that of water, enabling self-suspension in fracturing fluids, while crush rate (2.9% at 52 MPa) and acid solubility (3.3%) meet established industry benchmarks for ultra-lightweight proppants.

[164] Taken together, the material and modeling contributions establish a coupled experimental-transport framework in which sustained tracer release, governed by matrix-diffusion kinetics, is quantitatively linked to the observed breakthrough signal and, ultimately, to per-interval production rates. The framework is applicable to acid fracturing, deep-well, and high-pressure scenarios in unconventional reservoirs. We emphasize, however, that the present validation is limited to laboratory-scale single-interval experiments using dodecane as a model oil. Extending this approach to field-scale, multi-interval, multiphase conditions with crude oil—and establishing the corresponding operational reliability—represents the necessary next step toward practical deployment.
