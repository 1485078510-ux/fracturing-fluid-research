=== [7] ===
Sustained growth in global energy demand has positioned unconventional oil and gas resources—including shale oil, tight oil, and coalbed methane—at the center of contemporary petroleum development [1-4]. These resources now account for more than 50% of the world's total hydrocarbon reserves [5], and their efficient development is critical to global energy security. Hydraulic fracturing, the core stimulation technology for low-permeability and tight formations, creates artificial fractures that substantially enhance hydrocarbon seepage pathways and well productivity [6].

=== [9] ===
A persistent operational challenge follows stimulation, however: the oil production contribution of each fractured interval varies markedly, and interval productivity declines at different rates as field development proceeds [7]. Without reliable per-interval production data, reservoir productivity assessment and subsequent development strategy optimization lack a quantitative foundation, ultimately compromising the overall hydrocarbon recovery factor. Accurate, interval-level production monitoring is therefore not merely desirable but essential for the economical development of unconventional resources.

=== [11] ===
Among available diagnostic techniques, tracer-based methods offer a uniquely practical route to per-interval production monitoring: they require no permanent downhole instrumentation, can be deployed during routine fracturing operations, and provide direct chemical evidence of fluid contributions from each labeled interval. Conventional oil-soluble chemical tracers, however, exhibit limited compatibility with water-based fracturing fluids and cannot sustain long-term monitoring [8-12]. Tracer proppants—composite particles that immobilize tracer agents within a solid carrier and are co-injected with proppant-laden fracturing fluids—overcome these limitations by eliminating the need for separate tracer-injection operations. Zhao et al. [13] coated rhodamine 6G onto polyvinyl alcohol-modified ceramic particles to retard aqueous release; Zhou et al. [14] encapsulated carbon quantum dots in polymer-coated ceramic particles; Li et al. [15] employed rare-earth tracers with ammonium polymethacrylate coatings; and Gong et al. [16] embedded oleophilic Fe3O4 tracers into polystyrene (PS) microspheres for oil-phase monitoring. Despite this progress, all of these studies share a critical methodological gap: they characterize tracer release using empirical kinetic models—predominantly the Korsmeyer-Peppas power law—but none establishes a quantitative, physically grounded linkage between the release kinetics measured in batch experiments and the resulting tracer breakthrough signal observed at the wellhead. The advection-dispersion transport of released tracers from the proppant pack through the production tubing to the sampling point remains unmodeled. Consequently, per-interval oil production rates cannot be quantitatively inferred from tracer concentration histories, regardless of how accurately the release kinetics themselves are characterized. This transport-to-signal gap represents the central unresolved challenge in tracer-proppant-based production monitoring.

=== [13] ===
Compounding this methodological gap, the materials employed in existing tracer proppants face their own practical limitations. Coated proppants suffer from high particle density that impedes transport in fracturing fluids, and monitoring ceases abruptly once the polymer coating dissolves. Pure PS microspheres, while enabling oil-phase tracer incorporation, exhibit inadequate thermal stability and mechanical strength under downhole conditions [17-20]. Epoxy resin—a thermosetting polymer whose highly cross-linked network imparts outstanding thermal stability (decomposition temperature exceeding 350 degC), exceptional chemical resistance, and tunable mechanical properties—offers a compelling alternative matrix material [21,22]. Direct emulsion-polymerization synthesis of epoxy resin microspheres further avoids the coating-uniformity and interfacial-debonding risks inherent in multi-step coating processes, enabling low-density tailoring, nanoparticle modification, and in-situ tracer encapsulation within a single synthetic step. Li et al. [23] and Wei et al. [24] demonstrated that epoxy matrices can serve as carriers for water-soluble tracers in aqueous-phase inflow profiling. Extending this concept to the oil phase requires rendering the epoxy matrix oleophilic—so that it releases its tracer payload selectively into the oil rather than the aqueous phase—and, critically, integrating the material with a predictive transport model that translates the resulting tracer signals into quantitative per-interval production allocation. Neither the oleophilic epoxy tracer matrix nor its coupling with such a model has been reported.

=== [15] ===
Here we address this dual gap. First, we develop a piecewise advection-dispersion (ADE) model with smooth hyperbolic-tangent (tanh) transition that decomposes the tracer breakthrough curve into two physically distinct components: a Gaussian pulse representing the advective-dispersive transport of the tracer slug accumulated during the well shut-in period, and an erfc tail representing the sustained, matrix-diffusion-controlled release of residual tracer from the polymer carrier. This decomposition establishes a direct quantitative relationship between the measured tracer concentration history and the per-interval oil production rate, transforming parameters that would otherwise remain as empirical fitting coefficients into physically interpretable quantities. Second, to validate this model we designed and synthesized an oleophilic tracer proppant (ESP-T) in which stearic acid-modified nano-Fe3O4 (nano-Fe3O4@SA) is encapsulated within an epoxy resin matrix via emulsion polymerization. We systematically characterize ESP-T's microstructure, thermal stability, wettability, oil-water transport selectivity, and mechanical integrity; quantify its temperature-dependent release kinetics via the Korsmeyer-Peppas model; and validate the integrated material-model framework through single-phase and steady-state two-phase core displacement experiments. Together, this work establishes a coupled experimental-modeling methodology that translates sustained tracer release signals into quantitative per-interval production allocation—a broadly applicable framework for unconventional reservoir management.
