Among available diagnostic techniques, tracer-based methods offer a uniquely practical route to per-interval production monitoring: they require no permanent downhole instrumentation, can be deployed during routine fracturing operations, and provide direct chemical evidence of fluid contributions from each labeled interval. Conventional oil-soluble chemical tracers, however, exhibit limited compatibility with water-based fracturing fluids and cannot sustain long-term monitoring [8-12]. Tracer proppants, composite particles that immobilize tracer agents within a solid carrier and are co-injected with proppant-laden fracturing fluids, overcome these limitations by eliminating the need for separate tracer-injection operations. Zhao et al. [13] coated rhodamine 6G onto polyvinyl alcohol-modified ceramic particles to retard aqueous release; Zhou et al. [14] encapsulated carbon quantum dots in polymer-coated ceramic particles; Li et al. [15] employed rare-earth tracers with ammonium polymethacrylate coatings; and Gong et al. [16] embedded oleophilic Fe3O4 tracers into polystyrene (PS) microspheres for oil-phase monitoring. Despite this progress, all of these studies share a critical methodological gap: they characterize tracer release using empirical kinetic models, predominantly the Korsmeyer-Peppas power law [17,18], but none establishes a quantitative, physically grounded linkage between the release kinetics measured in batch experiments and the resulting tracer breakthrough signal observed at the wellhead. The advection-dispersion transport of released tracers from the proppant pack through the production tubing to the sampling point remains unmodeled [19-26]. Consequently, per-interval oil production rates cannot be quantitatively inferred from tracer concentration histories, regardless of how accurately the release kinetics themselves are characterized. The Korsmeyer-Peppas framework [23,24] and classical ADE analytical solutions [25] provide well-established mathematical tools for describing release kinetics and solute transport, respectively; the preparation of oleophilic nano-tracers via stearic acid modification [21] and the sphericity standards for proppant evaluation [22] supply the material foundations. Integrating these elements into a unified, physically grounded production-allocation methodology has not been attempted. This transport-to-signal gap represents the central unresolved challenge in tracer-proppant-based production monitoring.
