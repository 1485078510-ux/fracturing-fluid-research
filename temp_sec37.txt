[126] The interpretation model is derived from the one-dimensional advection-dispersion equation (ADE): ∂C/∂t + v·∂C/∂x = D·∂²C/∂x², where v = 4Q/(πd²) is the mean flow velocity (cm/min) and D = α·v is the longitudinal dispersion coefficient (cm²/min), with Q the effective oil-phase volumetric flow rate (cm³/min), d the tubing inner diameter (cm), and α the longitudinal dispersivity (cm). For a semi-infinite domain with an instantaneous slug of tracer mass M injected at x = 0, t = 0, the classical ADE analytical solution [29] yields a Gaussian pulse: Crise(x,t) = (M/A)/sqrt(4π D t) exp[-(x - v t)2 / (4 D t)], which describes the advective-dispersive transport of the tracer accumulated in the proppant pack during the well shut-in period. For a continuous source maintaining a constant concentration C0 at the inlet, the ADE solution takes the complementary-error-function form: Cfall(x,t) = (C0/2) erfc[(x - v t) / sqrt(4 D t)], which captures the sustained release of residual nano-Fe₃O₄@SA from the epoxy matrix after the main concentration pulse has passed. These two transport regimes—the shut-in slug and the matrix-diffusion-controlled tail—operate simultaneously during production. To construct a physically continuous composite model that avoids the non-differentiable discontinuity of a piecewise step transition, we blend the two analytical solutions via a hyperbolic-tangent weighting function w(t) = ½[1 + tanh((t - t₀)/σ)], where t0 is the crossover center and sigma is fixed at the sampling interval (4 min) to avoid an additional free parameter without clear engineering significance. The resulting piecewise advection-dispersion model—incorporating both the Gaussian pulse and erfc tail components with smooth tanh transition. The full composite model is then C(t) = cb + w(t)·Crise + [1-w(t)]·Cfall, with the tanh weight smoothly shifting dominance from the Gaussian pulse at early times to the erfc tail at late times, avoiding any artificial discontinuity in the breakthrough curve. The resulting piecewise advection-dispersion model is expressed as Eq. (2).

[146] The fitted parameters reward closer examination because several of them converge on the same physical picture from independent directions. The Peclet number Pe = x/α = 0.934 places the transport regime at the transition between advection-dominated and dispersion-dominated behavior, precisely what one would expect from a gradually releasing, matrix-diffusion-controlled source. Pure advection would produce a sharp, temporally compressed Gaussian pulse; the comparable dispersion broadens the peak and generates the extended tail that carries 47% of the integrated signal. That this near-unity Pe emerges independently from the ADE fit, while the K-P analysis in Section 3.6 independently points to non-Fickian anomalous transport, is a convergence that strengthens confidence in both analyses. The tanh blending formulation provides the mathematical vehicle for this two-component decomposition: a piecewise step-function transition would introduce a non-physical discontinuity in the first derivative of the concentration profile at the crossover, whereas the continuously differentiable tanh weighting preserves the physical interpretability of each component while ensuring a smooth composite curve.

Several fitted quantities carry operational significance beyond the curve-fitting exercise itself. The independently recovered flow rate (0.46 mL/min) agrees with the pump-set value (0.50 mL/min) within 8%, and the fitted mean residence time (MRT = 37.4 min) matches the convective travel time (x/v = 38.6 min, ratio 0.967). These concordances demonstrate that the model parameters are not merely empirical coefficients: they correspond to physically measurable quantities and can be recovered from the tracer signal alone, an essential property for field applications where independent flow-rate measurements may not be available for every interval. The 47% erfc tail contribution has direct practical relevance: it confirms that nearly half of the detectable tracer signal originates from sustained matrix-diffusion-controlled release rather than from the initial concentration pulse, establishing that ESP-T-based monitoring is fundamentally a diffusion-governed, extended-duration process. This conclusion, inaccessible from empirical release fitting alone, directly informs the design of sampling protocols and shut-in durations in field deployments. The fitted tail-plateau concentration cb, though treated as a constant here, reflects the steady-state diffusion flux sustained by the thermally activated release process documented in Section 3.6 (K increasing from 0.055 at 30 °C to 0.196 at 120 °C), suggesting a pathway for temperature-dependent model parameterization in multi-temperature field scenarios.

Several limitations should be noted. First, the piecewise ADE model assumes a single fractured interval and uniform proppant-pack properties; multi-interval interactions and pack heterogeneity may introduce deviations in field applications. Second, the tracer flux calibration relies on steady-state flow; during transient flow regimes (e.g., well start-up, shut-in, or rapid drawdown), the relationship between FO and oil-phase flow rate may not hold. Third, the laboratory-scale validation used dodecane as a model oil; crude oil compositional effects (asphaltene adsorption, viscosity variation) on tracer release kinetics require further investigation. Fourth, at temperatures exceeding 120 °C or in the presence of aggressive formation fluids (high salinity, CO₂, H₂S), the long-term chemical stability of the epoxy matrix and the integrity of stearic acid surface modification warrant additional evaluation. These limitations delineate the current applicability envelope.

[154] The engineering value of this modeling approach lies in its ability to close the loop between a wellhead concentration measurement and an operationally actionable production-rate estimate. The fitted effective flow rate Q, independently validated against the pump-set value, serves as a direct proxy for the oil-phase flow contribution of each labeled interval, transforming what was previously a qualitative concentration trend into a physically grounded, verifiable production metric. The decomposition into Gaussian-pulse and erfc-tail components further distinguishes two information channels. The shut-in accumulation slug is sensitive to shut-in duration and tracer loading; the sustained tail reflects the intrinsic release characteristics of the proppant and persists without repeated shut-in operations. Because the model parameters are physically constrained, they can be cross-validated against independent operational data, pump-set flow rate, wellbore geometry, and transport time scales, providing an internal consistency check that increases confidence in the interpreted production allocation.
