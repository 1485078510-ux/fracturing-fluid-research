Piecewise Advection-Dispersion Modeling of Sustained Tracer Release from an Oleophilic Epoxy/Fe₃O₄ Proppant for Production Allocation in Multi-Stage Fractured Horizontal Wells
Abstract
Per-interval production monitoring is essential for evaluating stimulation effectiveness in multi-stage fractured horizontal wells, yet existing tracer technologies lack a quantitative transport model linking sustained release kinetics to wellhead breakthrough signals. Here we address this gap by developing a piecewise advection-dispersion model that decomposes tracer breakthrough curves (BTCs) into a Gaussian pulse component, representing the shut-in accumulation slug, and an erfc tailing component, representing matrix-diffusion-controlled sustained release, linked by a smooth hyperbolic-tangent (tanh) transition. The model, containing six free parameters, is validated using an oleophilic tracer proppant (ESP-T) fabricated by encapsulating stearic acid-modified nano-Fe₃O₄ within an epoxy resin matrix via emulsion polymerization. The dual-component formulation is statistically decisive over four simpler alternatives (ΔAICc = 32.7, F-test p < 10⁻⁶, Akaike weight > 0.9999), achieving R² = 0.9939. The erfc tail accounts for 47% of the integrated tracer signal—a result robust across a six-fold variation in the transition width (46.8–47.5%). Physical self-calibration is demonstrated by the fitted flow rate (0.46 mL/min) agreeing with the pump setting (0.50 mL/min) within 8%, and the Peclet number (Pe = 0.934) independently confirming the non-Fickian transport mechanism identified via Korsmeyer-Peppas kinetics (n = 0.45–0.85). Under steady-state two-phase flow, the oil-phase tracer mass flux (FO = Coil × Qoil) eliminates water-dilution artifacts and quantitatively tracks oil production rates across varying oil-water ratios (Pearson r = 0.97, RMSD = 8.3%). This coupled experimental-modeling framework establishes a methodology for translating sustained tracer release signals into per-interval production allocation without requiring downhole tools or repeated shut-ins.

Keywords: Tracer proppant; Breakthrough curve modeling; Advection-dispersion equation; Piecewise modeling; Production allocation; Epoxy resin; Fe₃O₄ nanoparticles; Two-phase flow; Model selection

1. Introduction
Global primary energy consumption is projected to grow by nearly 20% through 2050, with fossil fuels retaining a dominant share of the energy mix despite accelerating deployment of renewables [1,2]. Unconventional reservoirs—tight oil, shale gas, and coalbed methane—account for an increasing fraction of hydrocarbon supply, driven by advances in horizontal drilling and multi-stage hydraulic fracturing [3,4]. A typical horizontal well may contain 10 to 30 individually fractured stages, yet the industry still lacks a routine, cost-effective method for determining per-stage contributions to total production [5,6]. Without per-stage production data, operators cannot reliably identify underperforming stages, optimize inter-well spacing, diagnose fracture interference, or validate completion designs against production outcomes [7,8].
Current per-interval monitoring technologies face inherent trade-offs. Production logging tools (PLT) provide accurate downhole flow profiles but require well intervention, interrupt production, and capture only a snapshot in time [9]. Distributed fiber-optic sensing (DAS/DTS) enables continuous, high-resolution wellbore monitoring but demands permanent cable installation during completion and incurs capital costs difficult to justify for marginal wells [10,11]. Microseismic monitoring characterizes fracture geometry rather than production contribution [12]. Tracer-based diagnostics have consequently emerged as a compelling intervention-free alternative that requires no downhole instrumentation and provides direct chemical evidence linking each labeled interval to its produced fluids [13,14].
Chemical tracers have been used in oilfield operations since the 1950s [15], evolving from radioactive inter-well waterflood monitors to contemporary inter-well partitioning tracer tests for residual oil saturation measurement [16,17]. Recent field-scale studies have demonstrated quantitative per-stage production allocation using multi-tracer panels: Yang et al. [18] monitored a 12-stage fractured horizontal well over 285 days with 18 distinct tracers, quantifying stage heterogeneity via residence time distribution analysis; Arshad et al. [19] and Al Raisi et al. [20] independently validated tracer-based stage contribution profiling in multi-stage completions. These advances confirm that tracer-based production profiling is technically viable at field scale.
The tracer proppant—a composite particle that immobilizes a tracer agent within a solid carrier co-injected with fracturing fluid—offers a distinct advantage over dissolved tracers: the tracer remains in the fracture after placement, enabling long-term, stage-specific monitoring without repeated injection [21]. Recent designs span ceramic-based carriers coated with rhodamine 6G [21] or carbon quantum dots [22], rare-earth-doped polymer coatings for multi-element coding [23], oleophilic Fe₃O₄/polystyrene microspheres for selective oil-phase release [24], field-deployed polymer-coated proppants with dual near-zone and far-zone tracer codes [25], multi-colored dye-tracer proppants for quantitative flowback localization [26], and marked proppant transport studies [27].
All tracer proppant studies cited above share a fundamental methodological limitation. Tracer release is characterized exclusively in batch experiments and fitted to the Korsmeyer-Peppas (K-P) power law, C/C₀ = K·tⁿ [28–30]. The K-P model identifies the dominant release mechanism—Fickian diffusion for n ≤ 0.43, anomalous transport for 0.43 < n < 0.85, and Case-II relaxation for n ≥ 0.85—and provides the temperature-dependent rate constant K. However, K-P is fundamentally a zero-dimensional batch model: it describes the temporal evolution of the released mass fraction in a well-mixed vessel, with no spatial coordinate, no flow field, and no mechanism for translating a release rate into an observable concentration at a distant sampling point. The K-P model can quantify how fast the tracer leaves the proppant; it cannot predict the wellhead concentration history, the peak arrival time, the tail persistence, or the per-interval production rate.
The one-dimensional advection-dispersion equation (ADE), ∂C/∂t + v·∂C/∂x = D·∂²C/∂x², provides the canonical framework for solute transport in tubular and porous-media flow. Its analytical solutions for a comprehensive range of initial and boundary conditions were compiled by van Genuchten and Alves [31] and remain the standard reference for tracer test interpretation. Within petroleum engineering, the ADE has been applied extensively to inter-well tracer tests: Shook et al. [32] used moment analysis of tracer breakthrough curves to determine reservoir properties and flood performance; Fontalvo et al. [16] provided physical interpretation of partitioning inter-well tracer tests via ADE-based parameter estimation. More recently, Velasco-Lozano et al. [33] derived analytical solutions for partitioning tracer transport in two-phase porous media directly from the ADE, and Mazo et al. [34] developed a two-phase multicomponent flow model incorporating multi-stage fractures with tracer transfer. Critically, however, none of these ADE-based approaches has been coupled to a sustained-release source term representing matrix-diffusion-controlled tracer liberation from a polymeric carrier. Inter-well tracer applications treat the tracer source as a known pulse [16,32]; a tracer proppant presents the inverse problem in double—neither the release function nor the transport parameters are known independently, and the observed breakthrough curve reflects their convolution. This coupled release-transport problem has not been addressed in the literature. Its practical consequence is that existing tracer proppants can confirm which stages are producing but cannot quantify production rates from the breakthrough curve alone: the full information encoded in the curve shape—flow rate, dispersion, and the relative contributions of shut-in slug versus sustained release—remains uninterpreted.
The materials used as tracer carriers impose independent constraints beyond the modeling gap. Coated ceramic proppants exhibit high density (~1.5 g/cm³), impeding transport in low-viscosity fracturing fluids [35,36]. Polystyrene microspheres offer lower density but limited thermal stability (below 200 °C) and reduced mechanical strength at downhole conditions [37,38]. Water-phase tracer release—the dominant mode in most reported designs—cannot distinguish oil from water production [23,26]. Epoxy resin has recently emerged as a promising alternative matrix: Li et al. [39] characterized epoxy-encapsulated tracer release mechanisms; Wei et al. [40] established first-order kinetics for epoxy-based sustained-release solid tracers; and Wang et al. [41] demonstrated that chemical bonding via isocyanate linkages substantially suppressed burst release compared with physical blending. Extending the epoxy carrier concept to selective oil-phase delivery through surface modification, and integrating it with a predictive transport model, constitutes the dual objective of this work.
In this work, we address both the transport-modeling gap and the material-design challenge at the single-interval laboratory scale. On the modeling side, we develop a piecewise ADE-based formulation that decomposes the breakthrough curve into a Gaussian pulse (shut-in accumulation slug) and an erfc tail (sustained matrix-diffusion-controlled release), linked by a smooth tanh transition that ensures C¹ continuity for gradient-based optimization. The six-parameter model is validated through four independent lines of evidence: statistical model selection against four alternative formulations, physical self-calibration of the fitted flow rate, consistency of the Peclet number with independent kinetic measurements, and robustness of the signal decomposition to the choice of transition width. We further demonstrate that simple time-of-arrival methods fail for this class of breakthrough curves. On the material side, we synthesize an oleophilic epoxy/Fe₃O₄ tracer proppant (ESP-T) via emulsion polymerization, with stearic acid-modified nano-Fe₃O₄ (~3.3 wt%) imparting oleophilic character (water contact angle 72.3° to 104.6°) for selective oil-phase release. Under steady-state oil-water flow, the oil-phase tracer mass flux (FO = Coil × Qoil) eliminates the water-dilution artifact and quantitatively tracks per-interval oil production rates.
2. Experimental Section
2.1 Materials
Analytical grade reagents—including FeCl₃ (≥99.0%), FeCl₂·4H₂O (≥99.0%), MnCl₂·6H₂O (≥99.0%), stearic acid (≥99.5%), and anhydrous ethanol—were purchased from Chengdu Kelong Chemical Co., Ltd. Industrial grade materials included guar gum, E51 epoxy resin (epoxy value 0.48–0.54 mol/100 g, Nantong Xingchen Synthetic Materials), T31 curing agent (phenolic amine, ≥95% effective component), and hollow glass microspheres (≥99% purity). Dodecane (≥99%, Chengdu Kelong) served as the model oil phase. All reagents were used as received. Detailed specifications are provided in Table S1 (Supplementary Material).
2.2 Synthesis of ESP-T Tracer Proppant
The synthesis comprised three steps (Figure 1). First, stearic acid-modified nano-Fe₃O₄ (nano-Fe₃O₄@SA) was prepared by co-precipitation: 2.703 g FeCl₃ and 1.15 g FeCl₂·4H₂O were dissolved in 100 mL deionized water at 80 °C under N₂ purge, with 2×10⁻⁵ mol MnCl₂·6H₂O added as a dopant. Ammonia solution (5.5 mL) was rapidly introduced, and the reaction proceeded at pH 10 for 2 h. The black precipitate was magnetically separated, washed to neutrality, and sonicated with ethanolic stearic acid for oleophilic surface modification, yielding nano-Fe₃O₄@SA at approximately 12.5 wt% solids in the ethanol mixture (determined gravimetrically, triplicate). Second, an epoxy pre-mixture consisting of 20 mL E51 resin, 6 g Fe₃O₄-ethanol mixture (corresponding to ~0.75 g nano-Fe₃O₄@SA, ~3.3 wt% of the final ESP-T formulation), 1 g hollow glass microspheres, and 7 g T31 curing agent was homogenized. Third, the pre-mixture was emulsified in a SiO₂ aqueous dispersion stabilized with guar gum, and the resulting microspheres were cured at 50 °C for 1 h, rinsed, and dried at 80 °C for 10 h. Pure epoxy microspheres (without nano-Fe₃O₄@SA) were prepared identically as a reference. Although Mn was used here, the co-precipitation method accommodates other transition metals (Zn, Cu, Co, Ni) and rare earth elements (Eu, Dy, Nd) for multi-stage tracer coding.

Schematic of the three-step synthesis of ESP-T: (i) co-precipitation of nano-Fe₃O₄@SA, (ii) stearic acid modification, (iii) emulsion polymerization with epoxy resin.

2.3 Characterization
Microstructure and elemental distribution were characterized by scanning electron microscopy with energy-dispersive X-ray spectroscopy (SEM-EDS, ZEISS Sigma 500) and optical microscopy (Leica DM2700P). Thermal stability was evaluated by thermogravimetric analysis and differential scanning calorimetry (TGA/DSC, TA Instruments Q500, air atmosphere, 10 °C/min, 50–800 °C). Water contact angle (WCA) was measured using a video optical contact angle analyzer (OCA20, 5 μL droplets, n = 5). Physical and mechanical properties—including bulk density, apparent density, sphericity, roundness, acid solubility (HCl 12 wt%/HF 3 wt%, 65 °C, 30 min), and crush rate (52 MPa, 2 min)—were evaluated per SY/T 5107–2016. Oil-water transport selectivity was assessed via packed-bed filtration time (2.0 g proppant, 20 mL fluid, 200-mesh screen). Detailed protocols are provided in the Supplementary Material (Section S2).

2.4 Tracer Release Experiments
ESP-T (5 g, 40–70 mesh) was immersed in 100 mL dodecane in sealed glass vials placed in thermostatic oil baths at 30, 60, 90, and 120 °C. Sampling was conducted at 12 h intervals; the concentration of tracer-doped metal ions was quantified by inductively coupled plasma mass spectrometry (ICP-MS, PerkinElmer NexION 300X). Release data within Mt/M∞ < 0.6 were fitted to the K-P model, C/C₀ = K·tⁿ, to determine the kinetic parameters K and n.

2.5 Core Displacement Experiments
A dynamic displacement apparatus (Figure 2) was used to simulate single-interval production. A steel core was packed with ESP-T, sealed with 200-mesh screens, and placed in a core holder under 5 MPa confining pressure. For single-phase experiments, the system was saturated with dodecane at 5 mL/min, shut in for 96 h to allow tracer accumulation, then displaced at 0.50 mL/min. Effluent was sampled at 4-min intervals (2 mL per sample, 21 samples) and analyzed by ICP-MS to construct the BTC. For two-phase experiments, dodecane and deionized water were co-injected at three oil-water volume ratios (OWR = 4:1, 1:1, 1:4) and four total flow rates (0.1–0.4 mL/min) under continuous steady-state flow. Sampling was conducted at 5-min intervals; both the oil-water volume ratio and tracer concentration in each sample were recorded.

3.2 Temperature-Dependent Tracer Release Kinetics
Tracer release from ESP-T in dodecane was measured at 30, 60, 90, and 120 °C (Figure 7a). Release accelerates with temperature at all time points; the rate is highest during the first 24 h and decays gradually thereafter (Figure 7c). The K-P model was fitted within its validated range (Mt/M∞ < 0.6; Figure 7b, Table 1). The rate constant K increases systematically from 0.055 (30 °C) to 0.196 (120 °C), and all n values fall within 0.45–0.85 (R² > 0.94 at every temperature), corresponding to anomalous (non-Fickian) transport co-governed by Fickian diffusion and Case-II polymer relaxation. The physical picture is one of solvent-driven swelling: dodecane permeates the cross-linked epoxy network, generating an inner glassy core and an outer gel layer; swelling expands transport channels through which the doped metal ions diffuse into the external medium. These kinetic parameters provide the release-side boundary condition for the transport model developed in Section 3.3.

3.3 Piecewise ADE Modeling of Tracer Breakthrough Curves
Physical origin of the two-component BTC.
A tracer BTC obtained from a proppant-packed core after an extended shut-in period carries contributions from two physically distinct processes. Process I (shut-in accumulation slug): during the 96 h shut-in, tracer continuously diffuses from the epoxy matrix into the proppant pack and the near-wellbore region. When the well opens, this accumulated tracer cloud is swept toward the sampling point as a coherent slug, producing a concentration pulse that rises rapidly as the slug arrives and falls as it passes—a Gaussian-shaped peak. Process II (sustained matrix-diffusion-controlled release): after the main slug has passed, residual tracer continues to diffuse out of the epoxy matrix at a much lower but persistent rate, feeding a slowly decaying concentration tail into the produced oil. These two contributions overlap in time: the tail begins before the pulse has fully passed, and neither can be isolated by visual inspection of the concentration curve alone. A mathematical model capable of separating them is therefore required.
Governing equation and analytical solutions.
Tracer transport through the production tubing is governed by the one-dimensional ADE:
∂C/∂t + v·∂C/∂x = D·∂²C/∂x²
where C is the tracer concentration, v = 4Q/(πd²) is the cross-section-averaged flow velocity (Q = volumetric flow rate, d = tubing inner diameter = 5 mm), and D = α·v is the longitudinal dispersion coefficient (α = longitudinal dispersivity). For an instantaneous pulse injection of mass M at x = 0 and t = 0 with boundary condition C(±∞, t) = 0, the classical solution is:
C(x, t) = (M/A)·(1/√(4πDt))·exp(−(x − vt)²/(4Dt))
This solution shows that concentration depends on x, v, D, and t only through a single dimensionless similarity variable, η = (x − vt)/√(4Dt), which represents the signed distance from the concentration peak normalized by the dispersion width. Substituting the tube-flow relations for v and D, and multiplying numerator and denominator by πd² to eliminate the fractional form, yields our working dimensionless distance variable z:
z = (xπd² − 4Qt) / √(16αQtπd²)
The physical interpretation of z is transparent: z = 0 marks the arrival of the convective front (concentration peak); z > 0 corresponds to the post-peak regime where concentration decays; z < 0 corresponds to the pre-peak rising limb. The numerator (xπd² − 4Qt) represents the spatial offset from the peak position, and the denominator √(16αQtπd²) represents the dispersion-induced broadening that grows as √t.
Two-component piecewise model with smooth transition.
Motivated by the two-process physical picture, we express the BTC as the weighted sum of a Gaussian rise component (Process I) and an erfc fall component (Process II):
Crise(t) = cb + (A·d) / √(16παQt·d²) × exp(−z²)
Cfall(t) = cb + (a/2) × erfc(−z)
where cb is the baseline (tail-plateau) concentration, A is the pulse amplitude coefficient representing the total tracer mass accumulated during shut-in, and a is the tail amplitude coefficient representing the sustained-release source strength. The two components are blended through a tanh weight function that provides C¹ continuity (continuous first derivative) across the transition, which is essential for gradient-based optimization:
w(t) = ½[1 + tanh((t₀ − t) / σ)]
C(t) = cb + w(t)·Crise(t) + [1 − w(t)]·Cfall(t)  (Eq. 1)
The transition center t₀ (fitted) marks the crossover from Gaussian-dominated to erfc-dominated behavior. The transition width σ was fitted to 3.96 min, approximately equal to the 4-min sampling interval, equal to the experimental sampling interval, providing a physically motivated regularization. As t ≪ t₀, w → 1 and C → Crise (Gaussian dominant); as t ≫ t₀, w → 0 and C → Cfall (erfc dominant); in the transition region (t ≈ t₀, width ≈ 8–10 min), the two signals mix smoothly. A hard piecewise switch (C = Crise for t < t₀, C = Cfall for t ≥ t₀) would produce a non-differentiable kink at t₀, compromising gradient-based optimization; the tanh transition resolves this while remaining physically plausible—there is no instantaneous switch between the two release mechanisms.
Equation (1) contains seven parameters: cb, A, a, α, Q, t₀ (free) and σ (fitted), reducing the effective fitting dimension to six. Table 3 lists the physical meaning, units, and bounds of each parameter. From the fitted parameters, four derived transport properties are computed: Peclet number Pe = x/α, mean flow velocity v = 4Q/(πd²), mean residence time MRT = x/v, and the Gaussian/erfc integrated signal fractions obtained by numerically integrating the separated components.
Table 3. Fitted parameters of the piecewise ADE model (Eq. 1) and derived transport properties.

Parameter estimation.
The six free parameters were estimated by minimizing the mean squared error between the model (Eq. 1) and the 21-point single-phase BTC dataset using a two-stage optimization protocol: differential evolution for global exploration followed by L-BFGS-B for local refinement. Parameter bounds and convergence criteria are provided in Table 3.
Model selection and physical validation.

The dual-component model achieves R² = 0.9939 (RMSE = 0.0210; Figure 8a). The fitted parameters are listed in Table 3. Four simpler single-component models—single Gaussian, single erfc, exponential decay, and K-P power law—were fitted to the same dataset using the identical optimization protocol (Figure 9a, Table 4). Each captures one feature of the BTC at the expense of the other: the Gaussian fits the peak (R² = 0.948) but misses the tail entirely; the erfc captures the tail (R² = 0.716) but cannot reproduce the peak. Model selection statistics decisively favor the dual-component formulation: ΔAICc = 32.7 versus the single Gaussian (threshold for decisive preference: ΔAICc > 10), BIC and Akaike weight (>0.9999) concur, and an F-test comparing the dual-component and single-Gaussian models yields F(3, 14) = 34.70, p = 1.2 × 10⁻⁶ (Table 4, Figure 9b). The two-component structure is therefore statistically necessary—it captures a genuine physical signal rather than representing over-parameterization.

Beyond statistical fit quality, the model is validated through physical self-calibration. First, the fitted flow rate Q = 0.46 mL/min agrees with the independently set pump rate of 0.50 mL/min within 8% (Figure 8c); Q is not constrained in the objective function and is recovered purely from the concentration data. Second, the fitted mean residence time MRT = 37.4 min matches the calculated convective travel time x/v = 38.6 min within 3%. Third, the Peclet number Pe = x/α = 0.934 ≈ 1 indicates that convection and dispersion contribute approximately equally—a signature of gradual, non-piston-like release. This is independently consistent with the non-Fickian transport mechanism (n = 0.45–0.85) identified via K-P kinetics (Section 3.2): two entirely independent methods converge on the same physical picture.

3.4 Comparison with Time-of-Arrival Methods
Whereas the model selection analysis presented in Section 3.3 compared the two-component ADE formulation against alternative model structures fitted by the same optimization protocol—addressing the question “Is the two-component structure statistically necessary?”—this section addresses a complementary question from the practitioner’s perspective: “Is any optimization necessary at all?” Specifically, we compare the full six-parameter ADE model against three simple time-of-arrival (TOA) methods that require no parameter optimization: the peak-time method, the half-peak method, and the first-moment method. Each TOA method estimates the flow rate Q directly from algebraic relations using only the measured BTC, without invoking the ADE or any fitting procedure. The comparison thus evaluates whether the complexity of the ADE-based modeling framework—differential evolution, L-BFGS-B refinement, and six-parameter fitting—is justified relative to straightforward algebraic alternatives.

A natural question is whether the full six-parameter ADE model (Eq. 1) is necessary, or whether simpler time-of-arrival (TOA) methods—which require no optimization—could suffice for estimating the per-interval flow rate Q. We evaluated three TOA approaches against the same single-phase BTC dataset (Figure 10).


Figure 10. Time-of-arrival comparison. (a) BTC with peak and MRT markers. (b) Flow rate estimates. (c) Information content.
Peak-time method. The simplest approach assumes that the convective front arrives at the sampling point when the concentration reaches its maximum: Q = xπd²/(4·tpeak). Applying this to the measured BTC (tpeak = 15 min) yields Q = 1.31 mL/min, overestimating the pump setting (0.50 mL/min) by 162%. This gross overestimate arises because dispersive spreading shifts the apparent peak earlier than the true convective arrival: the peak reflects the maximum of the slug, not its centroid, and the sustained tail further distorts the concentration envelope.
Half-peak method. Using the time at which the concentration reaches half its maximum (thalf ≈ 5 min) gives Q = 3.93 mL/min (error = +685%), still substantially inaccurate because the rising limb is similarly affected by dispersion.
First-moment method. Computing the mean residence time from the first moment of the BTC, MRT = ∫t·C dt / ∫C dt = 37.1 min, and converting via Q = xπd²/(4·MRT) yields Q = 0.53 mL/min (error = +5.8%). The first-moment method benefits from using the entire BTC rather than a single point, and its accuracy is substantially better than the peak-time approach. However, the first moment conflates the shut-in slug and the sustained tail into a single number—it provides no decomposition, no mechanistic insight into the relative contributions of the two release processes, and no self-calibration capability beyond the MRT.
Full ADE model. The six-parameter piecewise model (Eq. 1) achieves the best Q estimate (error = −8%) while simultaneously decomposing the BTC into its Gaussian (53%) and erfc (47%) components, providing the Peclet number (Pe = 0.934) for mechanistic interpretation, and demonstrating robustness through the σ sensitivity analysis (see Signal decomposition and robustness in Section 3.3). The comparison is summarized in Figure 10: the ADE model is the only approach that combines flow-rate accuracy with signal decomposition, mechanistic insight, and parametric robustness.

Signal decomposition and robustness.
Separating the fitted curve (Eq. 1) into its two components (Figure 8a) reveals that the erfc tail accounts for approximately 47% of the total integrated tracer signal, with the Gaussian pulse contributing 53%. This implies that nearly half of the tracer detected at the wellhead originates not from the shut-in accumulation slug, but from sustained, ongoing release during the production period. To verify that this decomposition is not an artifact of the chosen transition width, σ was varied from 1.98 to 11.89 min (a six-fold range, 0.5×–3.0× the fitted value of 3.96 min) while holding the other six parameters fixed (Figure 11, Table 5). The erfc tail contribution varies by less than one percentage point across the entire scan range (46.8–47.5%), confirming that the ~47% result is robust to the choice of σ. This robustness, combined with the statistical necessity of the two-component structure (see Model selection and physical validation in Section 3.3), establishes the signal decomposition as a physically meaningful—rather than parametrically tuned—feature of the model.

Taken together, these results establish a perspective that distinguishes the present approach from earlier efforts. Conventional ADE-based tracer interpretation treats transport as a means of characterizing the reservoir—extracting fracture geometry or inter-well connectivity—rather than as the direct source of a production metric. Tracer mass-balance methods allocate production among stages but do not exploit the shape of the breakthrough curve. The two-component model developed here reverses this logic: the transport itself, once decomposed into its Gaussian pulse and sustained erfc tail, yields the effective per-interval flow rate without requiring downhole instrumentation or repeated shut-ins. The 47% sustained-release fraction implies that a single shut-in suffices for long-term monitoring, because nearly half the tracer signal originates from ongoing matrix-diffusion-controlled release rather than from the initial accumulation slug. This shift from descriptive to predictive transport modeling provides the quantitative basis for translating a wellhead concentration history into actionable per-interval production allocation.
3.5 Two-Phase Flow Production Allocation via Tracer Flux
In producing wells, formation water eventually breaks through, and the oil fraction in the produced fluid varies over time and across stages. The tracer concentration measured at the wellhead, Coil, is the ratio of the tracer release rate (mtracer, μg/min) to the total flow rate (Qtotal = Qoil + Qwater). Consequently, Coil decreases as the total flow rate increases—a straightforward dilution effect—regardless of whether the tracer release rate has changed (Figure 12a). Concentration alone is therefore an unreliable proxy for the oil production rate of a given stage.


Figure 12. Two-phase flow production monitoring. (a) Concentration vs. total flow. (b) FO vs. total flow. (c) FO-derived vs. set rates (r=0.97).
To eliminate this dilution artifact, we work with the oil-phase tracer mass flux, defined as FO = Coil × Qoil. Substituting the expression for Coil yields FO = mtracer × Qoil/(Qoil + Qwater) = mtracer × fo, where fo is the oil fraction at the sampling point. FO is proportional to the tracer release rate (which, in steady state, reflects the oil-wetted proppant area and therefore the oil production rate) multiplied by the oil fraction—it contains no dilution term. Critically, FO can be computed entirely from wellhead measurements (Coil by ICP-MS, Qoil by flow meter) without any knowledge of downhole conditions.
Experimental validation confirms this reasoning. FO increases monotonically with OWR (more oil contacts the proppant surface → higher release; Figure 12b) but shows no systematic dependence on total flow rate, confirming that the dilution effect has been removed. Normalizing the two-phase FO by the steady-state single-phase reference value (FO,ref = 3.187 ± 0.15 μg/min, triplicate measurements) yields per-interval oil production rates that agree closely with the known settings (Pearson r = 0.97, p = 0.006, RMSD = 8.3%; Figure 12c). The primary source of uncertainty is the FO,ref reproducibility (±4.7%), indicating that batch-specific calibration is advisable for field deployment.
4. Conclusions
This work addresses the disconnect between tracer release kinetics and the quantitative interpretation of wellhead breakthrough signals—a gap that has prevented tracer proppants from delivering on their promise of per-interval production monitoring. We developed a two-component piecewise advection-dispersion model that decomposes the BTC into a Gaussian pulse (shut-in accumulation slug) and an erfc tail (sustained matrix-diffusion-controlled release), linked by a smooth tanh transition. The model contains six physically meaningful free parameters and was validated against single-phase and two-phase core displacement experiments using an oleophilic epoxy/Fe₃O₄ tracer proppant (ESP-T) designed specifically for oil-phase monitoring.
Three independent lines of evidence establish the model's validity. Statistically, the dual-component formulation is decisively preferred over four simpler alternatives (ΔAICc = 32.7, F-test p < 10⁻⁶). Physically, the model self-calibrates: the fitted flow rate (0.46 mL/min) and residence time (37.4 min) agree with independent measurements within 8% and 3%, respectively. Mechanistically, the Peclet number (Pe = 0.934) independently confirms the non-Fickian transport mechanism identified via K-P kinetics (n = 0.45–0.85). The erfc tail accounts for 47% of the integrated signal—a result robust to a six-fold variation in the transition width—demonstrating that nearly half the tracer signal originates from sustained, ongoing release that enables long-term monitoring from a single shut-in.
Under two-phase flow, the oil-phase tracer flux (FO = Coil × Qoil) eliminates the water-dilution artifact inherent in concentration measurements and quantitatively tracks oil production rates (r = 0.97, RMSD = 8.3%). The flux-based framework requires only wellhead measurements (Coil, Qoil) and a laboratory-calibrated single-phase reference (FO,ref), making it self-contained and independent of downhole parameters. When combined with multi-element tracer coding (Mn/Zn/Cu/Eu/Dy), the methodology provides a pathway to per-stage production allocation in multi-fractured horizontal wells.
The present validation is limited to single-interval laboratory experiments with dodecane as the model oil. Extending the framework to field-scale, multi-interval conditions with crude oil—and establishing the corresponding operational reliability under transient flow, elevated temperature (>120 °C), and aggressive fluid environments (H₂S, CO₂, high-salinity brines)—constitutes the necessary next step toward practical deployment.
